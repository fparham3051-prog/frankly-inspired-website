The problem a leader names is often not the one holding the organization back. These notes show the difference: what leaders notice, what it usually turns out to be, and the first move to make.

Field notes

Franklin's observations from leading and advising growing organizations. These are patterns to check, not research findings.

More staff, more strain

Signal
The executive director is working longer hours, even after new hires.
Usual assumption
The organization needs more people.
Often it's
Decisions still run through one person. New staff add questions, not capacity.
Check
Which approvals land on the ED each week, who owns each workflow, and whether roles say what each person can decide alone.
First 90 days
List the ten approvals the ED makes most often and write down who decides each one from now on. Revisit the list in 30 days.

The second-site test

Signal
The first site has a waitlist, so leadership plans a second site or city.
Usual assumption
The second site needs a site lead and new funding.
Often it's
The first site's success lives in people's heads: how recruitment works, what partners agreed to, what a site really costs.
Check
Is there a written recruitment calendar, a partner agreement template, and a site budget model someone else could run?
First 90 days
Before hiring for site two, write site one's playbook: recruitment steps and dates, a standard partner agreement or MOU, and a budget template. Then test it by having someone new walk through it.

"We need a grant writer"

Signal
Proposals go out, but larger funders keep saying no.
Usual assumption
The organization needs better proposal writing.
Often it's
The evidence behind the story is thin. Outcomes are measured for one program, or none, and the numbers don't match from report to report.
Check
Outcomes tracked per program, the last two funder reports side by side, and whether a one-page logic model exists for the largest program.
First 90 days
Draft a one-page logic model for your largest program, pick one outcome, and start measuring it with every participant this quarter. Use the same number in every report.

    What the research shows

    The Transparency Premium

    In Candid's study of nearly 149,000 U.S. nonprofits, organizations displaying a Seal of Transparency received, on average, 62% more in contributions the following year than comparable organizations without one, after adjusting for size, financial health, age, and governance. The pattern held for small nonprofits too (61%).

    The Seal is free. It depends on how complete your public Candid profile is: mission, programs, financials, and leadership. Candid notes that a Seal is associated with higher giving, not a guarantee of it. Still, keeping your profile current is one of the cheapest trust signals a nonprofit can send. Funders read your public Form 990 the same way, so the governance answers there matter too.

    Source: Candid Research, "Study confirms nonprofit transparency: Candid Seals boost donor contributions"

    The First-Gift Cliff

    Nationally, roughly 4 in 5 first-time donors never make a second gift: first-time donor retention sits around 18%, well below the 43% overall average.

    A structured stewardship process in a donor's first 90 days is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost fixes most nonprofits have available.

    Source: Fundraising Effectiveness Project, Association of Fundraising Professionals & GivingTuesday

    The Recurring-Giving Advantage

    After one year, 71% of monthly donors are still giving, compared to just 24% of new one-time donors.

    Converting even a portion of one-time donors into monthly sustainers does more for long-term revenue stability than acquiring new donors at the same rate.

    Source: M+R Benchmarks, an annual digital fundraising study

    The Planned-Giving Gap

    Charitable bequests have made up roughly 7-9% of all U.S. giving in most years over the past four decades, and reached just over 10% in 2025, yet most nonprofits still don't have even a basic planned-giving program.

    At one studied institution, realized bequest gifts averaged 21 times a donor's lifetime annual giving, so a planned-giving conversation is also a marker of a donor's deepest capacity, not just a future line item.

    Source: Giving USA; the Chronicle of Philanthropy; PG Calc planned-giving research
